# Usability Testing
- Study should be planned carefully
- Formative Usability Studies
	- Low and medium fidelity
	- Learn from user and update design
	- measure *Effectiveness*, *productivity*, and *user satisfaction*
		- What issues did users have?
		- What worked well?
		- Did users have suggestions for additions or improvements
	- Like a quiz or midterm for your usability
- Summative Usability Study
	- High fidelity study
	- Final study before product is launched
	- Measure **how well the product meets the requirements**
	- Can still learn from users and apply learnings to new version of product
- Traditionally usability tests are moderated in a lab
	- Usually more than 1 moderator
	- Better for pre-release
- Online usability is better for post-launch
## Usability Metrics
- How much time and effort did it take to complete certain tasks?
- Compare user reactions to similar products and/or alternative designs
- Give a task and watch how the user completes the task
	- Number of clicks
	- Navigation routes
	- Frequency of feature use
- How noticeable are certain features
- How usable is the critical point
## Data Collection and Analysis
- Three core methodologies:
	1. Quantitative
		- Data is measurable
		- Collected from large number of participants(30+)
		- Usually via survey or focus groups
	2. Qualitative
		- Data is gathered via observation
		- Collection from fewer participants(>30)
		- Usually via interviews or observation
	3. Mixed-Method
		- Can use one method to create a baseline understanding and the other to cement the understanding
## Informed Consent Guidelines
- Should include purpose and procedure for the study
- Tell users how they will(or wont!) be anonymized
- Explicitly state risks
- Explicitly state the fact that participation is voluntary
- contact information
# UX
- The personal experience of a user using your product
- An emergent discipline
- An aspect of HCI
	- HCI Heuristics and principles create the foundation for a better UX
- User is always a stakeholder
- Three primary aspects:
	1. Usefulness
		- Is this needed? What is the advantage?
	2. Usability
		- How are users able to use the product?
		- Especially with accessibility
	3. Desirability
		- How much do users prefer this product over others?
## UI
- Design principles match those taught in traditional art/design school
- We use Cognitive Psychology laws and principles to help make better UIs
## Hick-Hyman Law
- Reducing the time to make a decision improves how well people interact with a decision
- The number of choices presented causes the decision time to increase logarithmically
- $ReactionTime=a+b*log_2(n)$
	- n is the number of stimuli(decisions)
	- a and b are measurable constants that depend on the task
- Practically:
	- Reduce number of choices
	- Create categories
	- Obscure complexity
## Gestalt Laws of Grouping
- Gestalt = Shape in German
- Refers to visual perception of shapes
- Most important aspects:
	- Proximity
	- Similarity
	- Continuity
	- Closure
	- Connectedness
- Proximity/Law of Common Region
	- People understand items close together as related, even if they're not
	- We should use containers/panels/boxes, etc., to group items quickly.
		- Creates clutter, don't overuse.
- Law of Simplicity
	- Inform the user with the least cognitive effort
	- Our brain will convert complex shapes into simple shapes
		- Therefore we should use the simplest form possible
	- **Simplify the design**
- Law of Closure
	- Our brain will fill in missing information; such as lines and text that isn't there.
		- The WWF panda is a great example
