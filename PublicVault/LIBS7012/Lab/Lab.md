# Statement/Claim
- Basic unit of "conversation" in logic
- Statement/Claim: A sentence that declares or asserts something about the world.
# Empirical Claims
- An assertion that something is or isn't a case
- Can be **absolutely** true or false, never in-between.
- Can be verified with empirical evidence.
- Other types of sentences should not be used within the argument.
	- Example: questions, rhetorical or not, cannot be true or false.
## Conceptual statement
- You don't need to observe the claim to validate it
- These statements are true or false *by definition*
	- e.g. "Some brothers have no siblings" - Brothers have siblings by definition, so it's false.
	- e.g. "Mammals are Animal" - Mammals are defined as animals, so it's true
# Normative Statements/ Value Claim
- Something *ought* or *ought not* to be true
- You need to declare a value/standard(a *norm*) to compare you statement against to find out if its true or false 
# Argument
- A set of Claims
	- One must be a **conclusion**
	- The others are **premises**
- Employed where there is a disagreement
## Inductive Arguments
> [!quote] If my evidence is true, my argument is likely true
- The majority of scientific evidence
- Allow for flexibility
- Do not give **certainty**
## Deductively Valid
> [!quote] If the premises are all true, the conclusion must be true
> Remember: You assume all premises are true!
- referred to as just "Valid" for short.
- e.g.: All cats are reptiles
	  Bugs Bunny is a cat
	  Therefore, Bugs Bunny is a reptile
## Normative Arguments
> [!note] Arguments that use one or more Normative Claims as a premise
- You cannot get an **empirical** truth from a **normative** argument.
- Keyword: "Should"/"Ought" rather than "Is"/"Do"
> [!quote] You cannot infer "Ought" from "Is"

# Utilitarianism
>[!quote] We should take a perfectly impartial 'view from the universe' when deciding what actions are right or wrong 
>-Henry Sidgwick
- Goal is to maximize 'good'
- Ideally, it maximizes good for maximum people, but it doesn't need to
- The equality of the decision is for the good of those **involved** in the situation
	- It is the same thing to maximize utility and minimize harm
- It is about finding a *specific action in a specific situation*
- Mill's version of Utilitarianism reminds me of Stoicism
## Perfect Impartiality
- The pleasure or pain of one person should be no more or less valuable than the pleasure or pain of another
## Perfect Symmetry
- There is no better or worse, or an action that is morally permissible in one situation and not in another.
- There is **only** right and wrong.
	- The one that causes more good is right, and the other is morally wrong.
- There is no moral difference between action and inaction - if your inaction causes more harm, then you are judged by the fact that you did nothing.
# Consequentialism
- "The ends justify the means"
- The only thing taken into account is the consequence
	- Nothing else is morally significant
# Kant
> [!quote] Awakened from his dogmatic slumber from the atrocity that was Hume.
> Happened when he was 40, hence his belief that you aren't a person until you are 40.
- Wanted to look at the idea of Duty and understand
	- What does it mean to have a duty
	- What makes a duty?
- He feared not having a way to hold people responsible and for them to be justified in their actions
	- We are responsible for our actions because our actions can change the world
	- Our actions can be motivated by our beliefs
	- Therefore we must be sure that our actions are just
> [!note] Beliefs is a "Propositional" belief
> - I believe that...
## Morally Worthy Actions
- Motivated by the belief that I have a duty to take the action
- Morally worthy duty is the duty to the **Moral Law**
- To get to Moral law, you need to act out of the "Good Will"
- Believes there was limited free will; 'Common Sense Free Will'
	- Free Will was "Contaminated" by external pressures; social, political, environmental pressures, etc.
	- If you remove all the "Contaminations", you get to the ability to make true choices; "Good Will".
	- tldr; Purge all the previous cause-and-effects from your choices to achieve Autonomy.
## Moral Agents
- Good Will allows us to become Moral Agents
- Since Morality is an aspect of Good Will, a Moral agent **is** a Good Agent
- Moral Agents ignore all Contaminated wills, so are Rational Agents
- Therefore a Rational Agent cannot act in the face of the moral laws
- If you take an action without moral reason, even if it right, it is Morally Worthless because you didn't do it for the right reason.

## Kantian Free Will
> [!quote] We can only **truly** be free if we resist our natural impulses and act on a law we have given ourselves.
- Common Sense Free Will is not good enough
- Absolutist; believed that if everyone could clear all of their Contaminated Wills, everyone everywhere would come to the **exact same Moral Law**
## Kantian Maxims
> [!quote] I ought never to act except in such a way that I could also will that my maxim should a universal law - Universality Formula
- Unlike normal Maxims, Kantian Maxims are **absolute**(Categorical Imperative)
	- You **ought** do X **if** you want y, rather than **if** you want x **then** y
	- This means if you want to **not** be responsible for an action, rather than doing something else, **you should do nothing.**
## Procedure for Kantian Maxims
1. Formulate your maxim
2. Recast that maxim as universal law that all Rational Agents **MUST** act on, rather than **OUGHT** to act on
3. Consider if your maxim is even possible in a world governed by this law. 
   If it is...
4. Ask if you would, or could, **rationally** will to act on your maxim in this world.
   If it is, your action is morally permissible.

## Eudaimonia
> [!quote] I do the right things, and I enjoy doing them
- Work cannot be intrinsically valuable
	- Work is something you do in order to gain leisure time
- Practical activities are **instrumentally** valuable, intellectual activities are **intrinsically** valuable
## Virtue
- To be Virtuous is to do the right thing, at the right time, because you enjoy it
- Aristotle: "A purposive disposition, lying in a mean that is relative to us, and determined by a rational principle"
	- An unvirtuous person can act in accordance with virtue by training/practicing to try to be virtuous until it becomes their nature
	- You can be Incontinent in 2 ways: 
		- Weakness: Knowing what to do, but lose the fight against a competing motivation
		- Impetuous: Acts without thinking and without conflict; e.g. a psychopath - ideally don't have the potential to become virtuous
- Hume: "Emotional disposition that is useful to oneself and others"
	- Focus on one's **true** interest; whatever your true needs as a human; close to Eudaimonia
	- "The kind of character trait that makes you loved and be worthy of being loved"
	- Traditional Catholic virtues are not real virtues because they are not useful
	- There cannot be a conflict between reason and emotion:
		- Motivation requires a belief(A reason) **and** a desire(an emotion)
		- Emotion tells you the destination and makes you want to get there, Reason tells you how to get there.
		- "Reason is a slave(an instrument) of the passions"
